# 08. Network Analysis

Network Analysis is the study of relationships and structural patterns within a network. It helps us understand how individual vertices, edges, and groups contribute to the overall structure and behavior of a network.

For example, in a social network, network analysis can help identify influential individuals, communities, and people who connect otherwise separate groups.

---

## 1. Centrality

Centrality measures help identify the importance or structural position of vertices within a network. However, "importance" can have different meanings. A vertex might be important because:

* It has many direct connections.
* It connects different parts of the network.
* It lies on many shortest paths.
* It is connected to other important vertices.

Different centrality measures capture these different aspects.

### 1.1 Degree Centrality

Degree centrality measures how many direct connections a vertex has.

For an undirected graph:
$$C_D(v) = \deg(v)$$

A normalized version is:
$$C_D(v) = \frac{\deg(v)}{|V| - 1}$$

#### Example:

```
        B
        |
A ----- C ----- D
        |
        E
```

Vertex $C$ has four direct connections. Therefore, $C$ has the highest degree centrality in this network.

* **Intuition:** How many people or entities are directly connected to this vertex?
* **Applications:**
  * Identifying highly connected individuals in social networks.
  * Finding popular products in customer interaction networks.
  * Identifying highly connected devices in communication networks.

### 1.2 Closeness Centrality

Closeness centrality measures how close a vertex is to all other reachable vertices in terms of shortest-path distance.

For a connected graph:
$$C_C(v) = \frac{|V| - 1}{\sum_{u \neq v} d(v, u)}$$

Where:
* $d(v, u)$ is the shortest-path distance between vertices $v$ and $u$.
* $|V|$ is the total number of vertices.

A higher closeness centrality means that a vertex can reach other vertices using relatively short paths.

* **Intuition:** How efficiently can information or resources travel from this vertex to the rest of the network?
* **Applications:**
  * Identifying locations that can efficiently distribute resources.
  * Studying information dissemination.
  * Finding centrally positioned nodes in transportation networks.

> **Note:** For disconnected graphs, closeness centrality requires a modified definition, commonly using reachable vertices and a normalization factor.

### 1.3 Betweenness Centrality

Betweenness centrality measures how frequently a vertex appears on shortest paths between other pairs of vertices.

$$C_B(v) = \sum_{s \neq v \neq t} \frac{\sigma_{st}(v)}{\sigma_{st}}$$

Where:
* $\sigma_{st}$ = Total number of shortest paths between vertices $s$ and $t$.
* $\sigma_{st}(v)$ = Number of those shortest paths that pass through vertex $v$.

If multiple shortest paths exist, the contribution is divided among them.

#### Example:

```
A ----- B ----- C
        |
        D
```

Vertex $B$ lies between the other vertices. It acts as a connecting point for several shortest paths.

* **Intuition:** How often does this vertex act as a bridge for communication between other vertices?
* **Applications:**
  * Identifying important intermediaries in social networks.
  * Finding critical routers in communication networks.
  * Detecting bottlenecks in transportation networks.

### 1.4 Eigenvector Centrality

Eigenvector centrality measures the importance of a vertex based not only on its number of connections, but also on the importance of the vertices connected to it. A vertex receives a higher score when it is connected to other high-scoring vertices.

The basic relationship is:
$$x_i = \frac{1}{\lambda} \sum_j A_{ij} x_j$$

In matrix form:
$$A x = \lambda x$$

Where:
* $A$ is the adjacency matrix.
* $x$ is the vector of centrality scores.
* $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue.

This connects network analysis directly with linear algebra.

* **Intuition:** Being connected to important vertices can make a vertex important itself.
* **Applications:**
  * Identifying influential accounts.
  * Ranking important web pages and entities.
  * Analyzing prestige within social networks.

### Comparing Centrality Measures

| Measure | What it captures |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Degree** | Number of direct connections |
| **Closeness** | Shortest-path proximity to other vertices |
| **Betweenness** | Participation in shortest paths |
| **Eigenvector** | Connections to important vertices |

> **Important:** There is no universally best centrality measure. The appropriate measure depends on what we mean by "importance" in a particular network.

## 2. Community Detection

A **community** is a group of vertices that are more densely connected to one another than to the rest of the network.

Consider a social network:

```
A ----- B          F ----- G
| \     |          |     / |
|  \    |          |    /  |
C ----- D -------- E
```

Vertices $A, B, C,$ and $D$ may form one community, while $E, F,$ and $G$ may form another. The connection between $D$ and $E$ links the two groups. Community detection algorithms attempt to identify such groups from the network structure.

### Why Community Detection Matters
* **Social Networks:** Finding groups of people with strong interactions.
* **Recommendation Systems:** Identifying users with similar interests.
* **Biology:** Discovering groups of interacting proteins.
* **Research Networks:** Identifying clusters of researchers working on related topics.

### Modularity

Modularity measures how strongly a network is divided into communities compared with a suitable random-network baseline.

A common modularity formulation for an undirected network is:
$$Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \left( A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \right) \delta(c_i, c_j)$$

Where:
* $m$ = Number of edges.
* $A_{ij}$ = Adjacency matrix entry.
* $k_i, k_j$ = Degrees of vertices $i$ and $j$.
* $c_i, c_j$ = Community assignments.
* $\delta(c_i, c_j) = 1$ when both vertices belong to the same community, otherwise $0$.

A higher modularity score generally indicates stronger community structure relative to the chosen null model. However, modularity is not a perfect measure and can have limitations, such as the resolution limit.

## 3. Assortativity

Assortativity describes whether vertices tend to connect to other vertices with similar characteristics. In other words, it examines patterns of similarity between connected vertices.

For example:
* People connecting with others of similar age.
* Researchers connecting with researchers from similar academic backgrounds.
* High-degree vertices connecting with other high-degree vertices.

### Types of Assortative Mixing

1. **Assortative mixing:** Vertices tend to connect with others having similar characteristics (e.g., highly connected people connecting with other highly connected people).
2. **Disassortative mixing:** Vertices tend to connect with others having different characteristics (e.g., high-degree vertices connecting with low-degree vertices).

### Degree Assortativity

Degree assortativity specifically examines whether vertices tend to connect to vertices with similar degrees. It is commonly measured using a correlation coefficient:
$$-1 \leq r \leq 1$$

| Value | Interpretation |
| :--- | :--- |
| $r > 0$ | Assortative mixing |
| $r < 0$ | Disassortative mixing |
| $r \approx 0$ | Little or no linear degree correlation |

* **Applications:**
  * Understanding social relationships.
  * Studying the structure of biological networks.
  * Analyzing connectivity patterns in technological networks.

## 4. Network Density

Network density measures how many edges actually exist compared with the maximum possible number of edges.

For an undirected simple graph:
$$D = \frac{2|E|}{|V|(|V| - 1)}$$

For a directed simple graph without self-loops:
$$D = \frac{|E|}{|V|(|V| - 1)}$$

A density of:
* $0$ means there are no edges.
* $1$ means every possible edge exists.

Density gives an overall picture of how interconnected a network is.

> **Note:** Density was introduced in Graph Properties. Here, it becomes useful as a network-level measurement when analyzing real networks.

## 5. Network Motifs

A **network motif** is a small recurring connection pattern that appears in a network more frequently than expected under an appropriate randomized-network baseline.

Consider a directed network. Some possible three-vertex patterns include:

### Feed-forward loop
```
A → B → C
 \_______↑
```
There is a direct connection from $A$ to $C$ and an indirect path through $B$.

### Mutual connection
```
A ⇄ B
```
Both vertices connect to each other.

* **Applications:**
  * Studying regulatory networks in biology.
  * Understanding communication patterns.
  * Discovering repeated structures in information networks.

## 6. Network Robustness

Network robustness describes how well a network maintains its connectivity and functionality when vertices or edges fail or are removed.

For example, consider a communication network with multiple alternative paths. If one connection fails, communication may still continue through another route. However, a network that depends heavily on a few critical vertices or edges may be more vulnerable.

Two common ways to study robustness are:
1. **Random failures:** Randomly remove vertices or edges and observe the network.
2. **Targeted attacks:** Remove important vertices or edges, such as those with high centrality.

Researchers often observe changes in:
* Size of the largest connected component.
* Number of connected components.
* Average shortest-path distance.
* Network efficiency.

Robustness connects directly with bridges, articulation points, and centrality measures.

## 7. Network Analysis vs. Graph Properties

These concepts overlap, but they answer different kinds of questions.

| Graph Properties | Network Analysis |
| :--- | :--- |
| Degree | Centrality |
| Degree distribution | Degree-based network structure |
| Density | Overall network connectivity |
| Clustering coefficient | Local group structure |
| Diameter | Network-wide distance |
| Connected components | Network fragmentation |
| — | Community detection |
| — | Assortativity |
| — | Network motifs |
| — | Robustness |

**Graph properties** provide measurements of a graph. **Network analysis** uses such measurements and additional methods to understand the structure, roles, and behavior of the network.

---

> **Key Takeaway:** Network analysis helps us understand more than just which vertices are connected. It helps us investigate:
> * **Importance:** Which vertices play significant roles?
> * **Position:** Which vertices are centrally located?
> * **Influence:** Which vertices connect to other important vertices?
> * **Groups:** Which vertices form communities?
> * **Relationships:** Do similar vertices connect with one another?
> * **Resilience:** How does the network behave when parts fail?
>
> The central idea is that a vertex's importance is not determined only by its individual properties, but also by its position and relationships within the entire network.
