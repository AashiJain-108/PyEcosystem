# 03. Graph Properties

Graph properties help us understand and measure the structure of a graph.

They describe how vertices are connected, how connections are distributed, and how close or far apart vertices are within a network.

We can study these properties at two levels:

- **Local Properties:** Characteristics of individual vertices and their immediate connections.
- **Global Properties:** Characteristics that describe the graph as a whole.

Let's explore them one by one.

## 1. Degree

The **degree** of a vertex is the number of edges connected to it.

It tells us how many direct connections a vertex has.

For an undirected graph:

$$
\deg(v)=\text{Number of edges incident to } v
$$

For example, if vertex $A$ is connected to $B$, $C$, and $D$:

$$
\deg(A)=3
$$

In an undirected graph, a self-loop contributes 2 to the degree.

### Degree in Directed Graphs

In directed graphs, we distinguish between incoming and outgoing connections.

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| In-Degree | Number of edges entering a vertex |
| Out-Degree | Number of edges leaving a vertex |

Mathematically:

$$
\deg^{-}(v)=\text{In-Degree}
$$

$$
\deg^{+}(v)=\text{Out-Degree}
$$

For example:

$$
A \rightarrow C,\quad B \rightarrow C,\quad C \rightarrow D
$$

For vertex $C$:

$$
\deg^{-}(C)=2,\qquad \deg^{+}(C)=1
$$

A directed self-loop contributes one to both in-degree and out-degree.

## 2. Neighborhood

The **neighborhood** of a vertex is the set of all vertices directly connected to it.

For example, if $A$ is connected to $B$, $C$, and $D$:

$$
N(A)=\lbrace B, C, D \rbrace
$$

The number of vertices in this set is called the neighborhood size.

$$
\vert N(v) \vert = \text{Number of neighbors of } v
$$

For a simple undirected graph:

$$
\vert N(v) \vert = \deg(v)
$$

Neighborhoods are useful when studying local relationships, finding nearby vertices, and understanding how a vertex interacts with its surroundings.

## 3. Degree Distribution

Degree distribution describes how vertex degrees are distributed throughout a graph.

Instead of examining the degree of just one vertex, we examine how many vertices have each degree.

For example:

| Degree ($k$) | Number of vertices |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 2 |

This tells us that:
- One vertex has no connections.
- Three vertices have exactly one connection.
- Four vertices have exactly two connections.
- Two vertices have exactly three connections.

The degree distribution can be represented using:

$$
P(k)=\frac{\text{Number of vertices with degree } k}{\vert V \vert}
$$

It helps identify whether connections are distributed evenly or concentrated around a few highly connected vertices.

This is particularly useful in social networks, communication networks, and large-scale network analysis.

## 4. Average Degree

The **average degree** is the average number of connections per vertex in a graph.

For an undirected graph:

$$
\langle k \rangle = \frac{1}{\vert V \vert} \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)
$$

Using the Handshaking Lemma:

$$
\boxed{\langle k \rangle = \frac{2 \vert E \vert}{\vert V \vert}}
$$

Where:

- $\vert V \vert$ = Number of vertices
- $\vert E \vert$ = Number of edges

For example, consider a graph with 5 vertices and 5 edges:

$$
\langle k \rangle = \frac{2(5)}{5} = 2
$$

On average, each vertex has two connections.

For a directed graph, the average in-degree and average out-degree are both:

$$
\frac{\vert E \vert}{\vert V \vert}
$$

## 5. Graph Density

Graph density measures how many edges actually exist compared with the maximum number of edges that could exist.

It tells us how interconnected a graph is.

### Undirected Simple Graph

$$
\boxed{D(G)=\frac{2 \vert E \vert}{\vert V \vert (\vert V \vert - 1)}}
$$

### Directed Simple Graph

For a directed graph without self-loops:

$$
\boxed{D(G)=\frac{\vert E \vert}{\vert V \vert (\vert V \vert - 1)}}
$$

Density ranges from 0 to 1.

| Density | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 0 | No edges exist |
| Close to 0 | Very few possible connections exist |
| Close to 1 | Most possible connections exist |
| 1 | Every possible edge exists |

A higher density indicates a more interconnected graph.

## 6. Distance Between Vertices

The **distance** between two vertices is the length of the shortest path connecting them.

$$
d(u,v)=\text{Length of the shortest path between } u \text{ and } v
$$

For an unweighted graph, distance is measured by counting the number of edges.

For a weighted graph, it is generally measured using the minimum total edge weight along a path.

For example:

$$
A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D
$$

If this is the shortest route from $A$ to $D$:

$$
d(A,D)=3
$$

If no path exists between two vertices, their distance is conventionally considered infinity.

**Why does it matter?**

Distance helps us understand how efficiently two vertices can be connected.

Applications include:
- Finding the shortest route between locations.
- Measuring separation between people in a social network.
- Understanding communication delays in networks.

## 7. Eccentricity

The **eccentricity** of a vertex is the maximum shortest-path distance from that vertex to any other vertex in the graph.

$$
e(v)=\max_{u \in V} d(v,u)
$$

For example, if the distances from $A$ to the other vertices are:

$$
d(A,B)=1,\quad d(A,C)=2,\quad d(A,D)=3
$$

Then:

$$
e(A)=3
$$

It represents the distance from a vertex to its farthest vertex.

For disconnected graphs, eccentricity may be infinite under the usual convention.

## 8. Diameter

The **diameter** of a connected graph is the maximum shortest-path distance between any pair of vertices.

In other words, it is the greatest eccentricity among all vertices.

$$
\boxed{D(G)=\max_{v \in V} e(v)}
$$

A smaller diameter means that even the most distant pair of vertices can reach each other through relatively few edges.

For example, if the greatest shortest-path distance between any two vertices is 4:

$$
D(G)=4
$$

## 9. Radius

The **radius** of a connected graph is the minimum eccentricity among all vertices.

$$
\boxed{R(G)=\min_{v \in V} e(v)}
$$

It represents the smallest possible maximum distance from a vertex to every other vertex.

A vertex whose eccentricity equals the radius is called a **central vertex**.

### Diameter vs. Radius

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Diameter | Greatest eccentricity in the graph |
| Radius | Smallest eccentricity in the graph |

For every connected graph:

$$
R(G) \leq D(G) \leq 2R(G)
$$

## 10. Clustering Coefficient

The **clustering coefficient** measures how closely a vertex's neighbors are connected to one another.

It helps us understand whether a vertex belongs to a tightly connected group.

For an undirected simple graph, the local clustering coefficient of a vertex $v$ is:

$$
C(v)=\frac{2 e_v}{k_v (k_v - 1)}
$$

Where:

- $e_v$ = Number of edges between the neighbors of $v$
- $k_v$ = Degree of vertex $v$

For a vertex with fewer than two neighbors, the local clustering coefficient is conventionally taken as 0.

### Example

Suppose vertex $A$ has three neighbors: $B$, $C$, and $D$.

Among these neighbors, the following connections exist:

$$
(B,C),\quad (C,D)
$$

There are 2 actual edges among the neighbors.

The maximum possible number of edges among 3 neighbors is:

$$
\frac{3(3-1)}{2}=3
$$

Therefore:

$$
C(A)=\frac{2}{3}
$$

A clustering coefficient closer to 1 indicates that a vertex's neighbors are more densely connected.

This property is especially useful in social network analysis, where friends of the same person may also be friends with one another.

## Quick Recap

| Property | What it measures |
|---|---|
| Degree | Number of connections of a vertex |
| In-Degree | Number of incoming edges |
| Out-Degree | Number of outgoing edges |
| Neighborhood | Set of directly connected vertices |
| Degree Distribution | Distribution of degrees across vertices |
| Average Degree | Average number of connections per vertex |
| Graph Density | Proportion of possible edges that exist |
| Distance | Shortest-path length between two vertices |
| Eccentricity | Maximum distance from a vertex to other vertices |
| Diameter | Maximum shortest-path distance in a connected graph |
| Radius | Minimum eccentricity in a connected graph |
| Clustering Coefficient | How interconnected a vertex's neighbors are |

## Why These Properties Matter

Graph properties are not just mathematical definitions. They provide the foundation for analyzing real-world networks.

For example, in a social network:

- **Degree** can help identify highly connected individuals.
- **Degree Distribution** can reveal whether a few individuals have disproportionately many connections.
- **Density** can describe how interconnected the overall network is.
- **Distance** can measure how many relationships separate two individuals.
- **Diameter** can indicate the maximum shortest-path separation in the network.
- **Clustering Coefficient** can reveal tightly connected groups of friends.

These measurements become building blocks for more advanced network analysis techniques.
